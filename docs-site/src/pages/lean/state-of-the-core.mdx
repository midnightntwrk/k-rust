## Coverage of K

As of 2026-11-13.

For this project K is a finite thing, at three levels: the KORE language (its sentence kinds, pattern constructors and attributes), the sentence shapes kompile 7.1.337 emits, and the hooks of K's builtin modules. The frontend's constructs (strictness, contexts, cells, macros) are compiled away by kompile and never reach the reader. Coverage is complete when every row is read, inert, or refused by a recorded decision.

| Part | rows | read | inert | refused by decision | parameter only | not handled |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| KORE: sentences, patterns, attributes | 66 | 38 | 13 | 4 | 0 | 11 |
| kompile's sentence shapes | 58 | 24 | 11 | 7 | 3 | 13 |
| builtin hooks | 317 | 66 | 0 | 50 | 97 | 104 |

- **read**: the reader turns it into part of the definition, and the kernel gives it its meaning.
- **inert**: classified and accounted for, with no meaning in the semantics.
- **refused by decision**: refused with a reason, and a recorded decision says it is refused on purpose.
- **parameter only**: the symbol is read and applied but has no interpretation in Lean, like Bytes and String; the theorems hold for every interpretation.
- **not handled**: refused or left unaccounted for, with no recorded decision.

## The core, layer by layer

The core is what you must understand to know what the theorems `Sound` and `Complete` say.
Everything else (every proof, the generator, the executable evaluator) is checked by Lean's kernel against it.

| # | Layer | What it says | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Terms | what a configuration is: ground terms, patterns with variables, sorts and symbols as numbers | settled |
| 2 | Signature | a definition as a Lean value: sorts, constructors, subsorts, functions as clauses with priorities, rules with priorities, collections, overloading symbols | settled, except the library of builtin sorts and symbols (it becomes a list of hook domains) and sort parameters (read by a special case for `ite`) |
| 3 | Values | which ground terms are values of which sort; configurations as well-sorted terms of the top sort | settled |
| 4 | Matching | when a pattern matches a value under a substitution, including map, list and set patterns (a set pattern that joins two sets matches a disjoint split) and patterns headed by an overloading symbol | settled |
| 5 | Evaluation | function evaluation as a relation: a clause applies where every clause of a smaller priority fails; the order of clauses means nothing | settled, except the builtin operations (hook domains: Bool and Int; Float, of which 25 operations are computed as IEEE 754 defines them and 16 are left uninterpreted; 35 String and Bytes operations, where whether an operation declared total but described on part of its arguments has a value there is not settled), silent points (the mechanism, with an empty table), and whether Bool operations are strict (decision 1 below) |
| 6 | Step | one step: a rule whose left side matches, whose condition is true, whose right side has a value, and no rule of a smaller priority applies | settled |
| 7 | What functions must do | four statements about evaluation the theorems assume: every function has a result, every `[total]` one a value, clauses of one priority agree, matches of one clause agree | settled |
| 8 | Matching logic | patterns, models and entailment, as much as the statements use | settled |
| 9 | The theory Γ | the theory a definition denotes, built from the Lean value: sorts, constructors, collections (four map axioms, five list axioms, four set axioms), overloading symbols (each overload an equation), function equations, rule axioms | settled, except the axioms of the builtin domains and of silent points (`ΓFallback`, empty today) |
| 10 | Sound and Complete | every step is entailed by the theory, which has a model; every successor the theory forces is a step; the two theorems, with their two hypotheses | settled |
| 11 | Well-formedness | the check on the semantics both theorems assume, six conjuncts: well-sorted subsorts, clauses and rules, the conditions the theory needs to have a model, names apart, and well-formed overloading symbols | settled, except two naming conditions: the first half of one (every symbol named like `ite` is named at a numbered sort), and that an overloading symbol is not named like `ite` at a sort |

## What you trust

1. Lean's kernel, its three standard axioms, and the declarations of Lean's core library the definitions rely on (287 declarations of Init, Std and Lean, 39 of them its `Float.Model`; no Mathlib).
2. The definitions the theorems are stated with: the step relation, evaluation, matching, the theory Γ and its step closure, the check on the semantics (`coreOK`), the four statements about functions (`FnHyp`), and entailment in matching logic. Reading the course is reviewing them.
3. The builtin domains: the Bool and Int operations, 25 Float operations and 35 String and Bytes operations, as defined in Lean. The other operations are parameters with no interpretation.
4. The reader of a KORE file, for a definition read without the tie: that the Lean definition it produces is the one the text describes. With the tie, the kernel checks that printing the definition back gives the text, up to the order of its sentences. Only peano is tied now.
5. kompile's translation from K to KORE. Nothing here speaks about the K source.

Not trusted: the generator, the deciders, the executable evaluator and k-rust. The kernel checks each against the definitions.

## Open decisions

Each is a question you can answer by replying.

1. **Strict Bool operations.** Today `A andBool B` has a value only when both `A` and `B` have one, and a definition that relies on short-circuiting carries an obligation per place it does. imp-sec's six such obligations are false, and parts of KEVM carry more, so those definitions would have to be rewritten with `ite` or guarded clauses. It no longer touches soundness or completeness. Keep the strict reading? (Recommended: yes.)
2. **A priority of 200 or above beside `[owise]`.** `[owise]` is priority 200, so a function with an `[owise]` clause and a clause of priority 200 or above is refused today. The KORE text puts the `[owise]` equation after every other equation, while the kernel reads `[owise]` as priority 200, and the two differ wherever both equations apply. Keep refusing, or read a priority of 200 or above as "after `[owise]`" and state where that differs from the KORE text?
3. **Definitions read without the tie.** The theory Γ is built from the Lean definition, and the tie, a kernel check that the definition prints back as its KORE text, is optional. Without it the reader is trusted. Accept that, or have a package read without the tie say so in the names of its theorems?
4. **The goal and its order.** Is the scoreboard above the end goal? The proposed order: read every definition from kompile's own KORE, starting with KEVM; rebuild one existing proof on a small package; review the core through this course; compare the executable evaluator with recorded outputs; then K in K.
5. **An `ensures` on a function equation.** One KEVM equation carries one. Proposed (and implemented): `ensures C` on a function equation is read as an extra `requires` condition, when all of the function's equations have one priority; otherwise the function is refused. Under that restriction the two readings agree wherever the text is consistent. Such a definition cannot be tied to its text. KEVM's one case meets the restriction. Agree? (A rewrite rule's `ensures` is now read the same way, where no rule of a larger priority overlaps the rule, and refused otherwise.)
6. **A `[total]` function that no equation covers at some arguments.** The kernel reads a function as what its equations say, so such a declaration is false and the definition cannot meet `FnHyp`. K's own theory instead says the function has some unspecified value there. Keep the strict reading, which means KEVM needs four small fixes, or read such a function as unspecified outside its equations? (Recommended: keep the strict reading.)
7. **KEVM as written.** Two pairs of its equations contradict each other, so no reading can prove it sound as written. Proposed: the KEVM row of the scoreboard counts as met for KEVM plus a short reviewed list of fixes (about nine equations), each listed on this page with its reason, and you decide whether to report them to KEVM's authors. Agree?
8. **What a set pattern `SetItem(X) REST` matches.** kompile's text states that joining sets is idempotent and does not require the two parts to be disjoint. So against a set that contains `X` the pattern matches twice: once with `REST` the set without `X`, and once with `REST` the whole set. Reading the two parts as disjoint gives one match, but contradicts the text's own idempotence axiom. 16 corpus texts have such a pattern.

   The evidence: K's own sources contradict themselves on sets. The one `idem` attribute states the union reading; the prose of `domains.md` ("nilpotent"), the definition of `|Set` and a definedness rule state the disjoint one. Both backends match disjointly, one match per member (evidence only). Real definitions rely on disjointness: KEVM never joins a set where the element may be present, and the corpus's member-removal and cell-set update rules depend on `REST` lacking the element.

   Recommended, and now the core's reading: joining is a partial, disjoint concatenation, as for maps. Agree?
