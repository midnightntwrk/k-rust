# What you have to read

Lesson 6 of [A K definition in Lean](/lean).

[Lesson 5](/lean/proving-a-property) ended with nine theorems the kernel accepted.
A skeptical reader still has a question: how do you know the proved statements are about the semantics of `peano.k`, and say what you wanted?
The kernel answers part of it, and the rest is a short, computed list of what to read.

The answer has four parts:

1. The Lean kernel checks every proof against its statement, so proofs need no reading.
2. What must be read is the statements and everything their meaning depends on.
3. Lean computes that set; it is not chosen by hand. For peano it is {numbers.total} lines, of which {numbers.once} are the same for every definition.
4. Everything else is either trusted or checked by the kernel.

## The cone, computed

The set of declarations a statement depends on is computed by a Lean command, `#review_cone`.
From a list of roots it follows the types of reached declarations and the values of reached definitions, does not enter proofs, and stops at Lean's own `Init`, `Std` and `Lean`.
For every declaration it prints its kind, its source lines and the declaration that first reached it; per module, the distinct lines covered.

<ConeScene />

An upper bound that does not trust the command: the eight modules it reaches import only `Init` and each other, so everything the statements can mention is in those eight files or in `Init`.

## What to read

<ReadScene />

### Once, for every definition

The {numbers.once} shared lines answer five questions about what a kompiled K definition means: its fixed sorts and constructors, its content, its configurations, matching and evaluation, and one step.
[Lesson 4](/lean/what-a-step-means) went through them on peano.
They are read once; another definition reuses them unchanged.

What to check against K while reading them:

- Matching does not check variable sorts. For a well-sorted rule this is sorted matching, because in a well-sorted term every position's sort is fixed by its context.
- The evaluation takes the first matching clause. If two clauses could both match, K's meaning would be the conjunction of two equations; the well-formedness check rules overlapping clauses out, so at most one matches and their order does not matter.
- A step rewrites the whole configuration, because kompile has already turned each rule into one on the whole configuration.
- Any literal is a domain value of a builtin sort, so the configurations are a superset of K's and a statement over all of them covers K's.

### Per project: peano

The {numbers.perProject} per-project lines are peano's data ({numbers.module('PeanoCore')}), its names ({numbers.module('PeanoLibrary')}) and its statements ({numbers.module('PeanoProperties')}).
The names are abbreviations: check that each label is the one in the data and that the configuration shape is the one in the rules.

For the data there are two routes; either suffices.

- **Read it** against `peano.k`, as lesson 4 did. This needs a reader who knows how kompile compiles K: the as-patterns are kompile's sharing of a matched subterm, the counter-cell variable and the injections are kompile's.
- **Rely on the kernel.** The package proves, by kernel evaluation, that the definition is well formed, that the KORE generated from the data and its presentation is exactly the syntax tree of kompile's output for `peano.k` (no renaming, no normalization), and that this KORE is in the supported fragment, which compares up to the order of each module's sentences ([lesson 3](/lean/the-definition-as-a-value)). Every sentence of that KORE is either fixed text, the same for every definition, or generated from a field of the data, so its rules and equations are the data's rules and clauses written in KORE. The cost moves to reviewing the generator once: its cone is {numbers.emit} lines. The kernel check is heavy: its last run took {numbers.f0Seconds} seconds and {numbers.f0Gb} GiB of memory.

## The differential against the reference kompile

The trusted kompile is krust's.
To learn whether that choice matters for peano, the reference K kompile (7.1.337, Haskell backend) was run once on the same `peano.k` and its output compared with krust's.
This is evidence about krust's output, not a reason anything here is correct: kompile stays in the trusted list.

<DifferentialScene />

The extra constructor matters to statements in one way: since the fixed signature has `#KConfigValue`, "for all configurations" includes configurations whose k cell holds one.
A statement about every configuration therefore has to hold for those too; for peano they are stuck, so every statement on [lesson 5](/lean/proving-a-property) holds for them trivially.

## Not per project: Theorem A

The statements of the matching-logic track, which say that the reference step is sound and complete for the theory of the KORE text, are reviewed once and separately.
Their cone is {numbers.rootText} lines: the matching-logic patterns and semantics, the KORE syntax, the translation of KORE into matching logic, and the generator.

## What is machine-checked here

- The cone listings are the output of `#review_cone` at the recorded commit; the scene shows the file as written.
- That the Lean data is peano's KORE: kernel-checked (the second route above), in a long run.
- The differential: `parse` accepting the reference KORE, its core being peano's up to declaration order, the same step relation, and the reference KORE being outside the fragment are kernel decisions. That the extra configurations are stuck and unreachable for peano was checked by reading the two rules; the scene also shows Lean computing that one of them has no successor.
- The execution differential: run outside Lean, by a harness that evaluates the package's step and calls krust and the reference backend; its numbers are the harness's summary. Evidence, not proof.
- Trusted: the items in the first scene.

## Reproduce

In the kcore-spec package directory:

```sh
lake build                           # the library and its checks
lake env lean test/ReviewCone.lean   # about 3 s; writes the three cone listings
lake env lean test/F0Check.lean      # heavy: the kernel checks of the second route
```

The last line is heavy: its last run took {numbers.f0Minutes} minutes and {numbers.f0Gb} GiB of memory.
To review another definition, list its statement definitions as the roots of a new `#review_cone` line in `test/ReviewCone.lean`.
